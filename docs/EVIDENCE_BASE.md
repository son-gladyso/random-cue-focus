# Evidence base and claim boundaries

Last reviewed: 2026-08-27

## Decision summary

The original product hypothesis—frequent random audible cues followed by a mandatory 10-second microbreak—was not supported strongly enough for a default experience. The upgrade uses a lower-interruption hypothesis: state a goal, receive an occasional optional check, record current state, and apply a prewritten recovery action after distraction.

No reviewed study directly validates Random Cue Focus, its 12–18-minute default, or an adaptive timer as a treatment or guaranteed productivity tool. The interval is a conservative, user-adjustable product heuristic, and nonessential checks can be disabled for the whole session. It should be changed or removed if product data shows burden without user-valued benefit. Off-task self-reports never cause more frequent checks; high skip burden may only reduce frequency.

## Claim-to-feature matrix

| Evidence finding | Confidence for finding | Product decision | Remaining uncertainty |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Phone alerts alone can impair performance on an attention-demanding task. | Moderate; direct lab experiment | Notifications and foreground sound default off; prompts are skippable. | Effect size and tolerance vary across real work contexts. |
| Microbreaks more consistently improve vigor/reduce fatigue than cognitive task performance. | Moderate; meta-analysis with risk-of-bias limits | Removed guaranteed “brain reset” and performance language; the check does not pause focus time. | Longer breaks and specific activities may behave differently. |
| Brief goal deactivation/reactivation mitigated vigilance decline in one sustained-attention paradigm. | Low-to-moderate for this product; indirect lab evidence | Use sparse goal reorientation rather than enforced relaxation. | Does not establish an optimal consumer reminder frequency. |
| Resumption aids can improve accuracy and resumption lag after interruption. | Moderate; systematic review/meta-analysis of lab studies | Re-display the concrete session goal and an if–then recovery plan. | A short text goal is only one possible resumption aid. |
| Thought probes can measure mind wandering without necessarily improving task performance. | Moderate; direct lab evidence | Store responses as self-reports; never label them objective attention scores. | Probe frequency can influence reports and burden. |
| Thought-probe reactivity varies by task and field protocol. | Moderate; lab experiments plus EMA meta-analysis with high heterogeneity | Allow a whole session with zero checks; treat timeout/skip as burden, not failure. | No direct trial establishes an optimal timer-check frequency. |
| Implementation intentions improve goal attainment across heterogeneous studies, but modern bias-robust estimates are smaller than early estimates. | Moderate | Let the user write an explicit “if distraction, then next small action” plan. | Effects depend on a valued underlying goal; exact effects in this app and modern digital work are untested. |
| Retrieval practice and distributed practice improve learning under many conditions. | Moderate-to-high for learning interventions | Learning outcomes must require actual recall content and retention measurement; timer minutes are not learning. | Optimal spacing depends on retention interval and material. |
| Retrieval transfer is stronger for near changes than far transfer, and corrective feedback is heterogeneous. | Moderate | Require source verification/correction and delayed retention before marketing learning mode. | Immediate performance, confidence, and near transfer do not establish broad learning ability. |
| Self-regulated learning training produces small-to-moderate academic effects. | Moderate | Treat the timer as a scaffold for planning/monitoring, not the intervention itself. | More extensive training is generally stronger than a timer scaffold. |

## Explicit exclusions

The product and documentation must not use “left-brain/right-brain learner,” fixed learning styles, “dopamine detox/reset,” subconscious programming, clinical treatment, or guaranteed focus/learning claims. Marketing screenshots and store copy are governed by the same boundary.

## Research limitations

- Most direct evidence comes from laboratory tasks, education settings, or interventions that differ materially from this app.
- Several syntheses include heterogeneous designs and risk-of-bias concerns.
- Self-report can be useful for reflection but is not an objective measure of attention.
- A check can alter the state it asks about; response logs are not passive ground truth.
- Classroom retrieval evidence does not validate a timer-only “learning mode,” and broad or far-transfer claims remain out of scope.
- Absence of direct evidence is not evidence of no effect; it requires restrained claims and product evaluation.
- This review is not a medical or educational prescription and should be refreshed before claims or defaults materially change.
